# Evaluation and Conclusion

## 1. Answering the Research Question

The research question this dissertation set out to answer was whether large language models understand action and consequence moral valence in ways similar to human moral perception. The results in the previous chapter give a qualified answer: **partially, and unevenly across models, prompt styles, and the two valence dimensions.**

Under the best-performing condition tested (few-shot prompting), every model reached a CCC of at least 0.65 on Action Valence and 0.59 on Consequence Valence against the human annotations — moderate, not weak, agreement, and clearly better than chance. But no model came close to the very strong agreement (CCC above roughly 0.8–0.9) that would be needed to claim LLMs judge moral scenarios *the way a human does*, rather than *in a way that is correlated with, but distinguishable from,* how a human does. The honest conclusion is that these models have picked up a real, substantial amount of the pattern in human moral judgment — likely from the broad ethical and normative content present in their training data — without matching it closely enough to say they perceive moral valence the same way.

## 2. The Central Finding: Models Agree With Each Other More Than With Humans

The most interesting result in this dissertation was not originally the primary research question — it emerged from the Cross-Model Agreement analysis (Results §2). Both this analysis and the Human-LLM Alignment analysis in §1–2 are reported using the same primary metric, Lin's CCC, specifically so this comparison is fair: Pearson would have overstated the gap, since CCC is mathematically always ≤ Pearson for the same pair of series (Methodology §6). With that controlled for, every single pairwise CCC between two models (0.696–0.894) is still higher than every single model's zero-shot CCC alignment with the human annotator (0.527–0.695). This is not a small or borderline gap — the *weakest* model-to-model agreement observed (Llama–Claude, 0.696–0.755) is still higher than the *weakest* model-to-human alignment (Claude, 0.527/0.599), and every model-pair CCC exceeds the *strongest* model-to-human alignment observed under zero-shot prompting (Llama, 0.695 Action). Under few-shot prompting the human-alignment numbers rise to 0.59–0.72, narrowing the gap somewhat, but the ordering — models agree with each other more than any of them agrees with the human annotator — still holds throughout.

This is worth dwelling on, because it changes what "LLMs disagree with humans on ~30% of the pattern" actually means. If each model diverged from the human in its own, model-specific way, we would expect the models to also disagree with *each other* by a similar amount — five independent, idiosyncratic approximations of the same target should scatter around it fairly independently. That is not what the data shows. Instead, the five models — trained by five different organisations, on different data, with different architectures — converge on a shared way of scoring moral scenarios that is *more internally consistent than it is consistent with the human annotator*.

The most plausible explanation is that these models share more with each other, in how they were built, than any of them shares with any one individual human's moral judgment. All five are large-scale instruction-tuned or RLHF-aligned systems trained on broadly overlapping swaths of public text and refined with broadly similar techniques for producing "reasonable," non-extreme, socially acceptable answers — which is a plausible mechanism for a shared "LLM-typical" moral stance that differs systematically from any single human rater's stance, even if that individual difference is itself unremarkable. This dissertation cannot fully confirm this explanation — that would require comparing against *multiple* human annotators, not one (see Limitations, §4) — but the pattern in the data is consistent with it and is, at minimum, a genuine empirical finding rather than speculation: **on this dataset, "LLM consensus" and "human moral judgment" are measurably different things.**

## 3. Prompting Matters — and Matters More for Consequence Valence

Results §1 showed that few-shot prompting improved every model's alignment with the human annotator, on both valence axes, with no exceptions across the five models tested. Two things about this pattern deserve comment.

First, the *size* of the improvement varied a great deal by model — from a negligible +0.01 (Llama, Action) to a substantial +0.08 (GPT-4.1, Consequence) — which suggests models differ not just in their baseline alignment with human judgment, but in how much they benefit from being shown a small number of worked, correctly-labelled examples before answering. Llama's comparatively small improvement, on a model that already had the highest zero-shot Action CCC of the five, is worth noting on its own: it hints that a model closer to its post-training ceiling on this task has less room left for a few examples to move the needle, though confirming this would need testing across a wider range of prompting strategies than this dissertation covers.

Second, the average improvement was consistently larger for Consequence Valence (+0.058) than for Action Valence (+0.044). One plausible reading is that judging whether an *action itself* was right or wrong draws more directly on the kind of general normative reasoning already well-represented in a model's training data, while judging whether an *outcome* was good or bad requires more scenario-specific, situational calibration — exactly the kind of calibration a handful of worked examples is best placed to provide. This dissertation reports the pattern; it does not claim to have proven this specific causal explanation.

## 4. Bias Testing: A Preliminary, Not a Conclusive, Result

The gender bias-testing results (Results §3) are the weakest-evidenced part of this dissertation, and are presented as such rather than overstated. Across the three models tested, the direct Male-versus-Female comparison — the pairing most directly relevant to a claim of gender bias specifically — did not reach the conventional p < 0.05 significance threshold for any model, on either Action or Consequence Valence, and the observed mean shifts were small and inconsistent in direction across models. **This dissertation does not claim to have found evidence of gender bias in these three models' moral valence scoring.** It also does not claim to have found evidence of its absence: with only 35 scenario pairs and three of the five models untested, this sample size and coverage cannot resolve the question either way. A substantially larger counterfactual dataset (several hundred scenario pairs, not 35) and evaluation of the remaining two models would be needed before a confident claim could be made in either direction.

## 5. Action–Consequence Divergence: Models Separate Judgment from Outcome

A further pattern emerged from manual inspection of individual results and was subsequently investigated systematically (Results §4): scenarios where all five models agree, often strongly, on whether the *action* in a scenario was right or wrong, while disagreeing — in some cases in direction, not just degree — on whether the *outcome* of that action was good or bad. 54 of the 500 zero-shot scenarios (10.8%) exhibit this pattern, and the effect is specific to the Consequence axis: across these 54 scenarios, cross-model spread on Action Valence sits at the dataset-wide average, while cross-model spread on Consequence Valence runs roughly 52% above the dataset-wide average.

This is a meaningfully different observation from the cross-model convergence finding in §2, and arguably a more interpretable one, because it isolates *where* the models' shared judgment breaks down rather than only measuring *how much* it breaks down overall. A representative case (Table 7, Results §4) is a scenario in which a teacher helped a student cheat on an exam: all five models score the action itself as clearly wrong, within a narrow band of one another, yet Llama and DeepSeek judge the consequence as net-positive while the other three judge it as net-negative — the same act, condemned identically, but its downstream effects read as good by two models and bad by three. A plausible explanation is that judging an action's rightness draws on a comparatively stable, well-represented normative rule ("cheating is dishonest"), whereas judging a consequence requires a causal, often speculative judgment about downstream effects — did the student benefit or suffer in the long run — on which models trained on different data, or applying different implicit assumptions about what happens next in an under-specified scenario, are more likely to diverge. This dissertation reports the pattern and this candidate explanation; confirming the explanation would require probing model reasoning text directly, which the framework already captures for every prediction but which was not systematically analysed for this purpose here.

## 6. Contribution

This dissertation's contribution is fourfold:

1. **An empirical answer, with real numbers, to how closely five contemporary frontier LLMs track a human annotator's moral valence judgments** — moderate agreement (CCC 0.53–0.72 depending on model, axis, and prompt style), improved but not resolved by few-shot prompting.
2. **A secondary, empirically grounded finding that these models agree with each other substantially more than they agree with the human annotator** — a result with a direct methodological implication for the field: model-vs-model agreement is not evidence of correctness relative to human judgment, and should not be treated as a proxy for it.
3. **A finding that model agreement is not uniform across the two valence dimensions** — models converge more reliably on judging an action than on judging its consequence, a distinction the source literature's combined-valence framing does not surface.
4. **A reusable evaluation architecture** (Methodology, Implementation) built so that Human–LLM Alignment, Cross-Model Agreement, and demographic Bias Testing are each independently computed, independently verified, and cleanly separable — meaning any of the three can be extended (more models, more scenarios, more demographic variants) without the other two needing to change.

## 7. Limitations

Stated plainly, for a reader who wants to know exactly how far these results can be trusted:

- **Single annotator.** All human labels come from one person (the author), following the source paper's annotation methodology but not using its published dataset. This is the single largest limitation of the whole study: "alignment with the human annotator" in this dissertation means alignment with one specific person's moral judgments, not with human moral judgment in general. A different annotator, or a panel of annotators with inter-rater agreement reported, would be needed to generalise this claim.
- **No statistical significance testing on the alignment/agreement correlations themselves.** CCC, Pearson, and Spearman values are reported as point estimates; confidence intervals or bootstrap resampling were not computed for them, only for the bias-testing Wilcoxon tests. A future iteration of this work should add this.
- **Small bias-testing sample.** 35 scenario pairs is enough to detect a large, consistent bias, but not a subtle one — see §4 (Bias Testing) above.
- **Only two prompting strategies compared in depth.** Zero-shot and few-shot were compared systematically; the interpretation note in Results §1 already flags that this should not be read as general evidence about prompt engineering strategies beyond these two specific configurations.
- **One model's sampling behaviour differs from the others.** Claude's API (as used here) does not expose a temperature parameter, so it was not evaluated at the same temperature=0 setting as the other four models — a real, if likely small, asymmetry in experimental conditions across models.
- **Not all models were evaluated on the gender bias-testing dataset.** Llama and GPT-4.1's results are absent from Results §3 at the time of writing.

## 8. Future Work

- Extend the bias-testing dataset well beyond 35 pairs, and to all five models, to give the significance testing real statistical power.
- Recruit multiple human annotators and report inter-rater agreement, to separate "alignment with this specific annotator" from "alignment with human moral judgment" as a more general claim.
- Add confidence intervals (e.g. via bootstrap resampling) to every correlation-based metric reported in this dissertation.
- Investigate the cross-model-convergence finding (§2) directly, by testing whether it holds for models from the same organisation/training lineage more strongly than across organisations — which would be a more direct test of the "shared training/alignment recipe" explanation offered above.
- Analyse the reasoning text captured for the 54 Action–Consequence Divergence scenarios (§5) directly, to test the candidate explanation that consequence judgments diverge because models make different implicit causal assumptions about an under-specified outcome.
